## What matters for exports (10 lines)

1. The current US tariff on Brazil is the Section 301 25% plus the forced-labor 12.5% (37.5%), not the IEEPA 50%. It is on a firmer legal footing and does not expire on its own (A21–A22).
2. Only 16.5% of Brazil→US exports (US$6.6bn) face the full 37.5%. 52.7% are exempt, including coffee, beef, OJ, aircraft, pig iron and most pulp (A23).
3. Machinery, wood, footwear, furniture, apparel and oils are the exposed manufactures. Steel and aluminum sit under Section 232, which a Brazil deal would not remove (A23, A24).
4. Brazil's US exports fell 6.6% to US$37.7bn in 2025, and H1-2026 was down 13%. Crude oil (-30%) and semi-finished steel (-22%) drove the fall (A13, A15).
5. The 2025 tariffs were openly political (the Bolsonaro trial). The 2026 tariffs are framed as trade (301), but Rubio blamed Lula personally (A01, A25).
6. Flávio blames the tariff on Lula ("tarifa do Lula") and promises bilateral deals to zero tariffs. A Flávio win plausibly lowers the 301/forced-labor layer; Lula IV likely keeps the stalemate (A28, A25).
7. The Flávio downside risk for China trade: he floated leaving BRICS and wants OECD accession. On China he says "pragmatic", and his program is silent on BRICS and Mercosur (A29–A31).
8. Russia channel: the new Graham Act secondary tariff (≤100%, from 18 Oct 2026) targets the top-5 buyers of Russian **crude/gas**. Brazil mainly buys Russian **diesel** and fertilizer, so the direct risk is low but not zero, and a waiver exists (A44–A48).
9. Russia supplies ~40% of Brazil's potash and was 59–81% of its diesel imports at times. A secondary-sanctions shock would hit farm costs and fuel more than exports directly (A46–A48).
10. The run-off is a coin-flip: polls are within the margin of error and Flávio led round 1 by 1.9 pp. Treat it as ~50/50 (A49, A50).
